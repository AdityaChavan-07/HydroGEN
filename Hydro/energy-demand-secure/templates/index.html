Idealized Stoichiometry & Carbonate Formation

Elemental-balance examples for an alkaline conversion concept—not validated kinetics or plant performance.

The equations below are idealized overall mass balances that illustrate one possible carbon-to-carbonate bookkeeping pathway. They do not establish the actual reaction mechanism or achieved yield. Carbonate formation is not, by itself, proof of permanent storage or zero life-cycle emissions; the source and recovery of NaOH, process heat and electricity, pretreatment, residual liquids, and eventual carbonate handling all matter.

Polyethylene (PE) Model [CH₂]ₙ Feedstock
CH₂ + 2 NaOH + H₂O ──> Na₂CO₃ + 3 H₂
  • Molar Mass: 14.027 g/mol
  • Idealized stoichiometric ceiling: 0.4312 kg H₂ / kg PE (assumes complete conversion)
  • Illustrative 70% assumption: 0.3018 kg H₂ / kg PE (not a measured plant yield)
  • Hydrogen LHV represented: about 36.2 MJ/kg plastic at that assumed yield
Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) C₁₀H₈O₄ Feedstock
C₁₀H₈O₄ + 20 NaOH + 6 H₂O ──> 10 Na₂CO₃ + 20 H₂
  • Molar Mass: 192.146 g/mol
  • Idealized stoichiometric ceiling: 0.2098 kg H₂ / kg PET (assumes complete conversion)
  • Illustrative 70% assumption: 0.1469 kg H₂ / kg PET (not a measured plant yield)
  • Hydrogen LHV represented: about 17.6 MJ/kg plastic at that assumed yield
Important mass-balance limit: the idealized PE equation consumes 2 mol NaOH per mol CH₂ repeat unit—about 5.7 kg NaOH per kg PE, before any excess reagent or recovery loop. The mass-balance example omits reagent production/recovery, heat demand, pretreatment, separations, and waste streams; it is not a process design or carbon-intensity result.

Carbonate Formation Assumptions & Proposed Roadmap

Carbonate product assumptions, system boundaries, and a proposed deployment roadmap.

The idealized PE equation implies a theoretical sodium-carbonate mass of about 7.57 kg per kg PE, but this is a stoichiometric calculation—not demonstrated saleable product output. Actual carbon may also remain in liquids, residual solids, or gases; carbonate purity, reagent recovery, end use, and permanence require validation. No CNT co-product or marketable-grade soda ash output is assumed in the cited bench study.

PHASE 1 (YEARS 1–3)

Decentralized Municipal Deployment

Deploy Small-Scale (1 t/d) and Mid-Scale (10 t/d) processing units directly at municipal sorting facilities and recovery centers. Minimizes feedstock transport while fueling municipal bus fleets.

CapEx: ₹11L to ₹1.16 Cr / Unit
PHASE 2 (YEARS 4–7)

Industrial Cluster Co-Location

Scale deployment to Large-Scale facilities (100 t/d) situated within industrial complexes, maritime ports, and chemical hubs. Direct H₂ injection into hydrotreating units and furnaces.

CapEx: ₹6.22 Cr to ₹7.15 Cr / Unit
PHASE 3 (YEARS 8–10+)

Macro Regional Energy Integration

Construct integrated multi-unit complexes (>1,000 t/d combined capacity). Connect directly to national hydrogen pipeline grids, H₂-DRI steelworks, and marine bunkering terminals.

Potential By-product Routes: Carbonate, Bicarbonate & CNTs

Possible downstream uses are separated from products actually demonstrated in bench-scale ATT.

Sodium carbonate (soda ash)

The supplied material lists potential uses in glass, detergents, paper, water treatment, and other industries. The idealized PE equation gives a theoretical ceiling of about 7.57 kg Na₂CO₃ per kg PE—not measured saleable output.

Sodium bicarbonate (downstream upgrade)

Not a direct ATT product. A separate carbonation step can convert soda ash using additional CO₂ and water: Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2 NaHCO₃. The CO₂ source, energy, and life-cycle balance must be assessed.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs)

The supplemental file proposes CNT recovery as a high-value option for batteries, composites, coatings, sensors, and membranes. This is an alternate carbon pathway—not a co-product established by the cited ATT study—and requires separate process evidence.

Carbon and market reality check: the supplemental file’s 4.45-billion-tonne/year soda-ash scenario is theoretical arithmetic based on applying the all-carbon-to-carbonate PE balance at macro scale. It is not an ATT production result or a market forecast. The cited bench study reported carbon distributed across inorganic, liquid, and gaseous/residual fractions (roughly 32–44%, 44–62%, and under 13%, respectively), so the assumption that all plastic carbon becomes saleable soda ash is not supported. CNT recovery would also compete for carbon that otherwise might form carbonate. Do not treat any by-product revenue or permanent storage as established without process, purity, market, and life-cycle evidence.

Potential By-product Routes: Carbonate, Bicarbonate & CNTs

Possible downstream uses are separated from products actually demonstrated in bench-scale ATT.

Sodium carbonate (soda ash)

The supplied material lists potential uses in glass, detergents, paper, water treatment, and other industries. The idealized PE equation gives a theoretical ceiling of about 7.57 kg Na₂CO₃ per kg PE—not measured saleable output.

Sodium bicarbonate (downstream upgrade)

Not a direct ATT product. A separate carbonation step can convert soda ash using additional CO₂ and water: Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2 NaHCO₃. The CO₂ source, energy, and life-cycle balance must be assessed.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs)

The supplemental file proposes CNT recovery as a high-value option for batteries, composites, coatings, sensors, and membranes. This is an alternate carbon pathway—not a co-product established by the cited ATT study—and requires separate process evidence.

Carbon and market reality check: the supplemental file’s 4.45-billion-tonne/year soda-ash scenario is theoretical arithmetic based on applying the all-carbon-to-carbonate PE balance at macro scale. It is not an ATT production result or a market forecast. The cited bench study reported carbon distributed across inorganic, liquid, and gaseous/residual fractions (roughly 32–44%, 44–62%, and under 13%, respectively), so the assumption that all plastic carbon becomes saleable soda ash is not supported. CNT recovery would also compete for carbon that otherwise might form carbonate. Do not treat any by-product revenue or permanent storage as established without process, purity, market, and life-cycle evidence.

Potential By-product Routes: Carbonate, Bicarbonate & CNTs

Possible downstream uses are separated from products actually demonstrated in bench-scale ATT.

Sodium carbonate (soda ash)

The supplied material lists potential uses in glass, detergents, paper, water treatment, and other industries. The idealized PE equation gives a theoretical ceiling of about 7.57 kg Na₂CO₃ per kg PE—not measured saleable output.

Sodium bicarbonate (downstream upgrade)

Not a direct ATT product. A separate carbonation step can convert soda ash using additional CO₂ and water: Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → 2 NaHCO₃. The CO₂ source, energy, and life-cycle balance must be assessed.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs)

The supplemental file proposes CNT recovery as a high-value option for batteries, composites, coatings, sensors, and membranes. This is an alternate carbon pathway—not a co-product established by the cited ATT study—and requires separate process evidence.

Carbon and market reality check: the supplemental file’s 4.45-billion-tonne/year soda-ash scenario is theoretical arithmetic based on applying the all-carbon-to-carbonate PE balance at macro scale. It is not an ATT production result or a market forecast. The cited bench study reported carbon distributed across inorganic, liquid, and gaseous/residual fractions (roughly 32–44%, 44–62%, and under 13%, respectively), so the assumption that all plastic carbon becomes saleable soda ash is not supported. CNT recovery would also compete for carbon that otherwise might form carbonate. Do not treat any by-product revenue or permanent storage as established without process, purity, market, and life-cycle evidence.
